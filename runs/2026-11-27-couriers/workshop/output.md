## Routing: what this mode is for, and what this specimen is

The operator command fires the mode. The mode's own routing rule points the other way, and the disagreement has to be stated before anything is run.

The mode deploys when the specimen is the user's draft, a workshopable draft, a fragment, a notebook piece, an in-progress poem, or a found text the operator wants in the jig. It routes to the standard sidecar when the specimen is a published complete work. This specimen is a published complete work by a dead poet. Every section of the form that names a next draft, a route, a trial, an experiment, or a peer in a thread is addressed to someone who is not there and cannot act.

Explicit operator command outranks an active mode's own routing under Extension Precedence, so the mode runs. It runs only where its sections describe what the page does. Where a section prescribes what a draft should do next, it is reported as inapplicable and left empty. Empty absence is the form's own permitted result, and a form filled by manufacturing faults in a finished poem would fail Checkpoint 6 and the mode's Murder-Incorporated and Craft-Part Policing failure modes at once.

The fitting route for this specimen is the standard sidecar, which reads reception, the layers, and Coscienza-reverse, and which this run does not produce.

One further thing the mode knows about itself applies here. The kernel's self-interference clause holds that the apparatus is an L3 rendering with its own load, and that the most useful thing a heavy apparatus can know is when it has become the noise. Thirteen sections on a thirteen-line poem is that case. The form permits a compressed treatment on a very short specimen, and this treatment is compressed.

## 1. Opening Reading

What is alive is a machine that breaks on schedule. Three times the poem holds up a small object as a question and then refuses it. Twice the refusal arrives with working grammar: a flat statement of ownership or authenticity, and a command, `Do not accept it.`, which stands at the close of L2 and opens L4 in the same four words. The third refusal, at L6, has no verb at all. It repeats one word, adds a conjunction and a noun, and stops. That is where the poem changes state, and everything after L6 is written in the grammar the third refusal was left with.

The main-trouble half of this section does not apply. There is no draft and no trouble to name. In its place, the thing a reading is likeliest to get wrong: L7 through L12 are not a fourth, fifth and sixth delivery to be refused. They are what arrives after refusal has run out of syntax.

## 2. Image Inventory

Three delivered objects, each at the head of its own question, each a small thing held on or inside something that contains it: L1, L3, L5. All three are posed, none is described, and none survives its couplet.

Four phenomena follow, in apposition and under no question: L7 to L8, L9 to L10, L11, L12. These are not held in containers. They are large, in motion, and unattached to any hand that brought them. One is counted and coloured at L10, the only number and the only colour in the poem.

The abstraction count is the finding, not a fault. The poem's first abstract noun is the last word of L6, at the exact point the refusal formula collapses. Before it, every noun is a thing. After it, the nouns are weather, geology, glass and water, and the poem's second and last abstraction is the first word of L13. The two abstractions bracket the second movement.

The form's instruction to name abstractions that need bodies is a draft instruction and is dropped. What the page shows is reported in section 7.

## 3. Sound and Cadence Scan

The poem has one rhyme and it is placed where the machine breaks. The noun closing L1, `leaf`, is answered by the noun closing L6. Nothing else in the poem rhymes, and the single rhyme joins the first delivery to the last refusal across the whole refusal sequence. The same noun from L1 returns at the opening of L7, unrhymed, on the other side of the break, which is the one word the poem carries across its own hinge.

Cadence. L2 and L4 are two short sentences apiece, end-stopped, level. L6 is three units in five words and is the shortest line in the poem. From L7 the sentence stops closing: the phrases run on across six line ends and reach no stop until L13.

The held-frame question before this section asked whether a poet reporting no ear is missing a faculty or running a borrowed prosody. No poet is present and none reports anything, so the question has no warrant on this specimen and did not fire. The scan ran at the phrase anyway, which is where a thirteen-line poem with two finite verbs rewards listening.

One pattern surfaced only in the scan and is reported as a finding about the scan rather than credited to the poem: a run of sibilants across L1 and L3. It did not register in reading order and does not enter the device inventory.

## 4. Line Break Analysis

Read at the declared unit, the line.

The breaks that earn their position are at L7/8 and at L12/13. L7 ends on an adjective and L8 opens on the noun it modifies, with the two words drawn from registers that do not belong together, one devotional and one folk. The break holds them apart for exactly one line end, which is the shortest interval at which a reader can feel the collision. L12 ends on a long dash and L13 begins the poem's only possessive claim since L2. The break is the whole distance between the last arriving thing and the speaker.

The breaks at L9/10 and L10/11 carry the poem's one grammatical suspension, a prepositional phrase split across the break so that the count and the colour arrive alone on their own line.

Test breaks are not suggested. There is no next draft to test them in, and proposing a break for a finished poem is Intention Substitution with a pencil.

Stanza-level break behaviour is not read. See the Rendering Note.

## 5. Diction and Language Check

The poem speaks in two vocabularies and does not blend them. The refusal sequence uses the language of a receiving clerk: ownership, acceptance, genuineness, and a flat verdict noun. Four words of it are a command anyone would recognise from a counter or a door. The second movement uses the language of weather, metal, chemistry and landscape, and issues no commands at all.

The one place the two touch is the L7/8 junction described above, where a word that belongs to devotion sits on one side of a break and a word that belongs to a kitchen or a story sits on the other.

The hunt for words trying to sound like work is a draft operation and is dropped. The relevant check is the parasitic-effort test, which runs at section 10 and comes back negative.

## 7. Voice and Moral Substrate

The voice comes from attention to what is handed over. The poem's whole position is reception: things are brought, inspected, and named false, and the speaker never produces anything until the last two words.

Where feeling is carried. Not in a body. There is no mouth, hand, throat, chest or breath in this poem. The two feeling-words are the abstraction at L6 and the abstraction at L13, and both are named rather than placed. The body-cost test fires weakly and its finding is that absence, which is patterned and goes to Checkpoint 9 and to the confinement below. It is not used as a fault. The workshop's own rule is not to force body detail where the poem does not give the opening, and this poem closes the opening deliberately: the refusals are issued by a judgment with no hands.

The cult-mimicry diagnostic is holstered. The poem issues imperatives, but it does not position its reader as supplicant and its difficulty is not acting as a gate: every sentence in it is sayable by a competent reader on a first pass, and what is withheld is the referent, not the access. Holstering a signature move is a completed judgment, and this is one.

## 9. Engine Bay

Current engine. The poem runs on authentication: things are brought to a speaker whose only power over them is to refuse them, and the power runs out before the deliveries do.

Load-bearing parts, named at extended-inventory resolution.

- Interrogation at L1, L3, L5. Three questions, none answered, each the frame for the object it carries.
- Imperative at L2 and L4. The same four words in both lines, once at a close and once at an opening.
- The abstraction spike at the end of L6, the poem's first non-thing.
- Apposition and parataxis from L7 to L12, carrying four items with no main verb between them.
- Dash break at the close of L12, which is the only punctuation in the poem that opens rather than stops.
- Epizeuxis at the head of L13.
- Withholding, in Category G, across the whole poem: the referent of the refused thing is never fixed.

Held-frame test. The poem repeats one shape without altering it and then alters it once. L1 and L2 set the shape: object-as-question, then a two-sentence refusal. L3 and L4 run it again with the two sentences swapped and the authenticity word changed, which is variation inside the frame rather than a break in it. L5 and L6 hold the question and move one element inside the refusal: the finite verb is removed. The frame is not resumed afterward.

The identity test is satisfied, and its thinness is reported honestly. Two recurrences establish the shape and a third alters it, which is the minimum that can be called a held frame at all. A reader who wanted more instances would not get them, and a shape merely similar would not count. What makes the call safe here is that the alteration is categorical rather than a matter of degree: the verb is present twice and absent once, and absent thereafter for seven lines. That is checkable by anyone with the page.

What the alteration converts. Before L6 the poem is a speaker exercising judgment. After L6 it is a page receiving arrivals. Had the verb stayed, the third refusal would have been a third verdict and the poem would have needed a different way out of its own list.

## 12. Workshop Verdict

Here is what the poem does at its best. It lets a refusal wear itself out in public. Two refusals come with working grammar and the third comes with none, and that is not a slip; it is the whole argument the poem has. Once the verb is gone the poem cannot say no again, and everything that arrives afterward arrives unjudged, which is a harder place to be than refusal. You can check this on the page in one pass: count the finite verbs. There are two, and they are both in the same stretch of four lines.

What it refuses to say. It never names who is sending, it never names what would have been genuine, and it never puts a body under either feeling-word. Those three absences are one absence, and the poem holds it from the first line to the last.

What a reading must not smooth. The last line is not a resolution handed to the reader. It is made out of the same doubling the last refusal was made of, and the poem gives you nothing to decide whether it is a seventh delivery, a seventh refusal, or the one thing taken. Closing that question is the reader's act, not the poem's, and a reading that reports the warmth and drops the undecidability has done the poem's work for it and kept the easier half.

There is no next-draft clause to this verdict. The poem is finished and the question of what it should trust next does not arise.
