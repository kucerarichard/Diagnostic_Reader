## 1. Opening Reading

Three things are alive here.

The title. `cab`, `drab`, `slab`, three rhymes inside seven words, and the articles dropped so it reads like a headline or a telegram. It is the one place in the piece where the words sound chosen rather than arrived at.

The slab itself. Not a stone, not a monument, a slab. Flat, cheap, cold. That is the right word for what you actually look at in a war cemetery, and the poem gets it in the first line and never improves on it.

L18. `wondering why`. It is the only place in twenty lines where the poem does not know something, and it is the only line that does not stop at its end.

The main trouble, plainly: the rhyme is choosing the sentences. Word order inverts to reach it, helping verbs pad the beat waiting for it, and at L4 it pulls in a verb that cannot take the subject it has been given. The second trouble is bigger and quieter. Twenty lines about a military cemetery, and the poem contains no proper noun. No place, no war, no name off the stone it tells us was legible.

## 6. Form Examination

Twenty lines, ten rhymed couplets, grouped four and four and four and four and four by where the sentences stop. A loose four-beat line.

The couplet is not the enemy. A plain rhymed couplet in a communal `we` is the right shape for a public memorial verse, and the piece belongs to that tradition honestly. What goes wrong is inside the shape rather than in the choice of it. The couplet closes each thought before the thought has been opened, so no idea survives into the next pair, and the rhyme word arrives before the sentence has decided what it wants to say.

Form is constraining the poem at four located sites: L4, L10, L11, L19. At every one of them the rhyme got there first.

## 12. Workshop Verdict

Here is what the poem does at its best. It gets in a cab, goes out to a field of graves, looks at the stone, and calls it a slab. Not a monument, not a memorial. A slab. That is the right word, it is the first thing on the page, and nothing in the next nineteen lines is as honest. Then twice more the poem gets out of its own way: once when the grass grows and grows past where the sentence wanted to stop, and once when soldiers go by in review and somebody is still wondering why.

Here is what it has not earned. Sadness, horror, pride, gallantry, and a vow that will never forget. Five feelings, all announced, none of them bought with an object. The poem stands in front of lettering it tells us was perfectly legible and does not read a word of it, and then tells us it felt sad. It goes out to a beach it cannot see from where it stands and reports a landing in a sentence that has lost its subject. And it rhymes on adverbs, which is where the whole thing comes apart: `gallantly` and `proudly` are not what the people in the cab saw, they are what the occasion handed them at the gate.

Here is what the next draft should trust. Trust the slab. Trust the grass. Above all, trust the one place where the line ran over the end and the poem admitted it did not know something. That hanging `why` is the real poem and it is currently being used as a rhyme word. Put one name on the page, the way the stone has it, and let the poem stop telling us how it felt.

What is this poem honest enough to say, and where is it still pretending to be a poem? Honest: that they went, that the stone was flat and cold, that the grass kept growing, that somebody was wondering why. Pretending: every line that reaches a feeling before it has found a thing.

This verdict runs at header fidelity. The register's calibrating corpus is withheld in this run by tier selection, and the voice has not been deepened from it.
